Alcove and Fences, honestly.

If you've gone looking for something to tidy up the icons on your Windows desktop, you have almost certainly found Stardock Fences first. It has been doing this since 2009, has passed twenty million downloads, and it is genuinely good. Alcove is at version 0.2.9 and was written by one person. So this page isn't a sales pitch dressed up as a comparison. It sets out what each one actually does, what Alcove doesn't do, and the cases where you should go and buy Fences instead. If you only take one thing away: they solve the same problem in two different shapes, and the shape matters more than the feature count.

01 The real difference Shape, not features

One shades the desktop. The other replaces it.

Everything else on this page follows from this one paragraph, so it's worth thirty seconds.

Fences

Regions drawn over Explorer's grid

Fences leaves the Windows desktop exactly where it is and draws shaded areas on top of it. Your icons are still Explorer's icons in Explorer's grid, just corralled. You can have as many fences open at once as you have room for, roll them up, flip between pages of them, and stack them into tabs. It is additive, it is conservative, and if it ever stops running you still have a normal desktop underneath.

Alcove

A desk that takes Explorer's place

Alcove paints itself over the desktop where Explorer's icon grid used to be and becomes the thing you're looking at. Icons live in named drawers on a rail, one open at a time, so your wallpaper spends most of its life empty. It reads your real Desktop folder and opens files through the real Windows shell, so nothing is copied or moved, but the surface itself is ours. Ctrl+Shift+F12 hands the desktop straight back to Explorer whenever you want out.

Which is to say: Fences is the safer, more conservative design, and we're not going to pretend otherwise. Alcove asks for more trust and gives you more room in return — a rail, a self-filling strip, a search window and a colour that follows your wallpaper, rather than boxes on a grid. It's also why the two can't sensibly run at the same time. Both of them want the desktop.

03 Buy Fences Five good reasons

When Fences is the right answer.

We'd rather you spent thirty dollars well than installed ours and were disappointed. Any one of these on its own is enough.

  • You want it to just work, today. Fences has had seventeen years of bug reports from twenty million machines. Alcove has had a few months. Our own download page says there will be rough edges we haven't run into yet, and we meant it.
  • You want roll-up, pages or tabs. Three genuinely good ideas, and Alcove has none of them. We haven't promised them, either.
  • A SmartScreen warning is a dealbreaker. Alcove isn't Authenticode-signed yet and Windows will say so the first time you run it. We explain why on the code signing page, but "it's explained" isn't the same thing as "it's signed".
  • You want serious auto-filing rules. Our Inbox catches everything new, and the first run offers to sort what's already there. That's a much smaller idea than Fences' rules engine, and if filing rules are the feature you're shopping for, Fences wins outright.
  • You already own Object Desktop. Then you already have Fences, and it fits with the rest of what you're running.

04 Try Alcove Five good reasons

And when we'd back ours.

Mostly these come down to price, source, and liking one drawer at a time better than a desktop full of boxes.

  • You want to read what owns your desktop. Alcove is MIT and the source is on GitHub. Something that starts with Windows and takes over the desk is a reasonable thing to want to read before you trust it.
  • Nothing to buy, key or activate. Every feature, every monitor, as many machines as you like. No licence to lose, and no activation server that can have a bad day and lock you out of your own desktop.
  • You'd rather see your wallpaper. One drawer open at a time, and the rail, strip and drawers tinted from the picture behind them instead of sitting on top of it as obvious panels.
  • You live on the keyboard. Ctrl+Space from anywhere in Windows searches icons, drawers, folders and running windows, which makes it an Alt+Tab you can type into, and > turns the same box into a command palette.
  • You want a way out. Ctrl+Shift+F12 hands the desktop back to Explorer immediately, and your whole setup is one JSON file you can delete.
