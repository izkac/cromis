DOC. 002  Alcove / what it is, and how it works Rev. 2026  ·  Version 0.2.4

Give every icon a home.

Let's be honest, your Desktop is where files go to be forgotten. Alcove swaps that loose grid for named drawers we call Alcoves. They open one at a time, spread out into a full canvas once they get crowded, and keep their own arrangement on every monitor you own. Each one borrows its light and its tint from the wallpaper sitting behind it. Underneath all that, Alcove is reading the real files on your Windows Desktop and opening them with the real Windows shell, so nothing gets copied or moved anywhere unless you move it.

01 What it does Four things

It organizes your desktop and leaves the rest of Windows alone.

Alcove paints itself onto the desktop where Explorer's icon grid used to be. Everything else about Windows carries on exactly as it always did.

1.1

It tidies up what you've already got

The first time you run it, Alcove has a look at your Desktop and offers to sort everything into Apps, Documents, Photos, Folders and Installers. Would you rather do it yourself? Then it starts with one empty Inbox and waits. Either way, the Inbox catches everything from then on. Alcove keeps an eye on the Desktop folder, so when a browser, an installer or an unzipped archive drops something in there, it turns up in the Inbox on its own a couple of seconds later. Same goes for anything you drag back out onto empty wallpaper.

1.2

One drawer open at a time

Click a tile in the rail and the drawer opens. Click it again and it closes. Opening a second one shuts the first, so your wallpaper spends most of its life uncluttered. Every tile shows the drawer's name and how many things are in it, and hovering over one tells you how much disk space it's quietly eating and which drawer is the worst offender. Want them in a different order? Drag a tile up or down.

1.3

It grows into a canvas when it needs to

A small drawer opens as a compact panel. Once it's carrying more than twelve things it spreads out across the desktop instead, where you can give the rows names and drag icons between them. If you prefer one shape over the other, the drawer header lets you pin it either way, and Alcove remembers.

1.4

A strip that fills itself

The frequent strip runs along the top or bottom edge and fills itself with whatever you actually open. You never have to curate it. Scores halve every couple of weeks so old habits quietly fade out, and a newcomer has to beat the weakest slot by half again before it's allowed to take the place. Once something has a slot it stays put even as the ranking shifts around underneath, so nothing ever slides out from under your cursor mid-click. Want one nailed down? There's Keep in this slot. Never want to see one again? You can ban it.

02 The look Straight from your wallpaper

It takes its colour from your wallpaper.

Before Alcove paints a single pixel it has a look at the picture behind it. A light wallpaper gets paper-coloured surfaces. A dark one gets slate. Both lean gently toward whichever hue dominates the image, by a small enough amount that you'd struggle to call it a colour. A mostly grey photo with one red car parked in it stays grey. A blue sky comes out blue.

The rail, the frequent strip and the open drawers all share one see-through wash, so they sit inside the picture rather than floating above it as obvious panels. Anything you actually have to read closely stays solid. Dialogs, right-click menus and the search sheet are dark enough to read comfortably on a dark wallpaper, and they will never blind you with glare-white on a light one.

How far all of this leans is up to you. Surface in Settings gives you Blend, Tinted (the default) and Solid, which paints the rail, strip and drawers as plain paper or slate. Text size scales every label at once, and Stronger text darkens the smaller ones for you. Since Alcove is the thing covering your desktop, its right-click menu is now where the wallpaper lives too. Background opens a picture picker over your own picture folders, sets what you click as the real Windows wallpaper across every monitor, or clears it for a flat colour. It stays put when Alcove is closed, and Alcove re-tints itself the moment you change it.

03 Search Four lists, one window

Ctrl Space from anywhere in Windows

It's a standalone window, so it comes up even when Alcove itself is buried behind other apps. Results are ranked by how often you actually open something rather than by how neatly the name matches, so the thing you use every day wins a close call. A weak match still won't beat a strong one, though, however often you open it.

3.1

Two lists before you type a letter

Today is whatever you've changed since midnight. Frequent is what you open most. Today holds back to two pictures at the most, so an afternoon of saving wallpapers can't bury the document you were actually writing. Press 1–9 and one opens without your hand ever leaving the keyboard. Under every name you get the type, the size and when it last changed, which is the whole difference between five files called pexels-70588695 and five files you can tell apart.

3.2

Type > and the list becomes verbs

New drawer, collapse every drawer, change the wallpaper, open Settings, hide or show the Windows taskbar, empty the Recycle Bin. That last one still goes through Windows' own confirmation, because we would rather not be the reason your files vanished. Each command sits beside the shortcut that already does it, so the palette gently teaches you how to stop needing it.

  • Icons, on the desktop and in every drawer.
  • Running windows. Pick one and you switch straight to it, which makes Ctrl+Space an Alt+Tab you can type into.
  • Drawers, by name. Enter opens the drawer on whichever screen it lives on.
  • Deeper in your folders. A drawer pointed at a folder only lists its newest files, so search goes and walks everything underneath as well. It gives up after about half a second and shows you what it found by then, nearest folders first, politely skipping the places nobody ever means: node_modules, AppData, the Recycle Bin.
  • Every group is labelled, so a window never gets mistaken for a file.
  • Ctrl+Enter shows the file in Explorer rather than opening it, and Shift+Enter opens the folder around it.
  • You'll never hit a dead end. A path you typed opens, a web address goes to your browser, a single word runs the way Win+R would, and there's always a web search waiting at the bottom.
  • It paints in the desk's colour, so it matches the wallpaper underneath from its very first frame.

04 Good behaviour Playing nicely

It's a good citizen on your machine.

A desktop shell that breaks the rest of your machine isn't worth a tidier wallpaper, so we've been careful here.

  • Real files, real shell. Alcove reads your actual Desktop folder and opens files with their normal Windows associations. The icon you clicked pulses while it loads, so even a slow launch looks like something is happening.
  • A drawer can mirror a real folder. Point one at Downloads or a project directory and it shows that folder's live contents: icon, list and details views, sortable columns, Backspace to go back up. You're looking at the folder itself, live.
  • A USB stick gets a drawer of its own. Plug one in and it turns up on the rail a second later, named after the drive, and browses like any other folder drawer. Its eject button flushes what is still being written and unmounts the drive; if something else has a file open the eject fails and Alcove says which drive it was. Unplugging closes the drawer, and none of them are remembered between runs. Not for you? One switch in Settings.
  • The taskbar keeps working. Your running apps stay exactly where they always were.
  • The Recycle Bin is still the Recycle Bin. Right-click it and you get Windows' own menu, just as you'd expect.
  • The wallpaper stays Windows' wallpaper. Set one from Alcove and it's the real thing, on every monitor, still there after Alcove is closed. Windows' own background settings are one menu item away.
  • Every monitor gets its own desk. Drawers belong to a screen and can be dragged over to another, and each desk saves its own layout.
  • Nothing moves unless you move it. Filing an icon into a drawer is Alcove's own bookkeeping. Your file hasn't budged.
  • One file holds your whole setup. Layout, drawers and pins all live in %APPDATA%\com.alcove.desktop\desktop.json. Delete it and you're back to a fresh desktop, no harm done.
  • There's an emergency exit. Ctrl+Shift+F12 hands the desktop straight back to Explorer, whatever else happens to be going on at the time.
  • No account, no telemetry. Alcove works fine with the network unplugged. The only question it ever asks the internet is whether a newer build exists.

The shortcuts worth knowing.

Dragging works about how you'd hope: drop an icon on a drawer to file it, on the corner pins to freeze it in place, on empty wallpaper to send it back to the Inbox, or on an app in the frequent strip to open it with that app instead of its usual one.

06 Licence MIT

Free software, in both senses of the word.

Alcove starts with Windows and effectively becomes your desktop. Something that sits that close to your machine ought to be something you're allowed to read.

Promise 1

Nothing to buy, nothing to key

Every feature, every monitor, on as many machines as you like. No licence key to lose, no time limit, no nag box at startup. There's nothing to activate and nothing that can expire on you.

Promise 2

Read it, change it, pass it on

MIT. Use it, modify it, build it, hand it around, ship it inside something of your own, commercially or otherwise. Keep the copyright notice and the rest is yours.

Promise 3

Nothing that can lock you out

No activation server, no phone-home, no check that can quietly fail on a bad day and leave you staring at an empty desktop. Alcove works with the network unplugged.

The source is on GitHub, so please do read it, learn from it, check what it's doing to your machine, and build it yourself if you'd rather. Updates are signed, and the app will refuse one that doesn't verify. Alcove is built on Tauri, React and Rust, each under its own licence.
